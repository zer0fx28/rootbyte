## The Modern Story

January 19, 2025: The US TikTok ban officially took effect. For approximately 14 hours, TikTok was unavailable on US app stores. The app showed a message: "Sorry, TikTok isn't available right now."

January 20, 2025: Donald Trump's inauguration. Within hours, Trump posted on Truth Social that he was issuing an executive order to delay the ban's enforcement. TikTok came back online.

March 2026: The legal and legislative situation remains unresolved. TikTok has 170 million US users. ByteDance still owns it. The "sell or ban" law is still technically in effect. Nobody knows what happens next.

The TikTok saga is the most visible front of a tech-driven geopolitical conflict that has been building since 2003.

## ROOT: Going Back to 2003

In 2003, two former eUniverse employees — Tom Anderson and Chris DeWolfe — launched MySpace from an office in Beverly Hills. MySpace was the first social network to reach mainstream adoption in the US, peaking at 100 million users in 2006.

MySpace's key insight — one that Facebook refined, and TikTok perfected — was that **user data was more valuable than user-generated content**. The posts, photos, and profiles were bait. The behavioral data — who you know, what you click, how long you look at things, who influences your decisions — was the product.

Rupert Murdoch bought MySpace for $580 million in 2005, convinced it was a media platform. He was wrong about what it was. It wasn't a media platform. It was a data collection system with a social network as the interface.

**The data value chain:**
- **2003** — MySpace proves user behavioral data has commercial value
- **2006** — Facebook's News Feed algorithm proves that engineered content ordering increases engagement (and data collection)
- **2012** — Instagram proves visual content + algorithmic recommendation = addictive loops
- **2016** — ByteDance launches Douyin in China (internationally released as TikTok in 2018)
- **2020** — TikTok reaches 1 billion users; US government flags data concerns for the first time
- **2024** — US Congress passes the Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act — the "sell or ban" law
- **2025** — 14 hours of ban, then un-ban; legal limbo continues

## Did You Know

The Chinese government classifies TikTok's recommendation algorithm as a **controlled export under China's technology export restrictions**. This means: even if ByteDance wanted to sell the algorithm to an American buyer, the Chinese government has the legal authority to block the sale. ByteDance can sell the brand, the user data infrastructure, and the app code. It cannot sell the algorithm without Chinese government approval.

The Chinese government has explicitly said it will not approve the algorithm sale. This is why every proposed "solution" (Oracle partnership, Walmart investment, government-supervised divestiture) has failed. The thing that makes TikTok dangerous is the same thing that makes it valuable — and Beijing controls access to that thing.

## Why It Matters Today

The TikTok debate is superficially about a social media app. It's actually about three overlapping conflicts:

**1. Data sovereignty** — TikTok collects the same behavioral data that every US social media company collects. The concern isn't what data is collected. It's who has access to it. The Chinese National Security Law (2017) requires Chinese companies to cooperate with intelligence requests from the government. ByteDance is a Chinese company.

**2. Algorithmic influence** — A recommendation algorithm that controls what 170 million Americans see and engage with is a potential information warfare tool. This isn't theoretical — researchers have documented that TikTok's content about China is systematically different between Chinese users (Douyin) and international users (TikTok) on politically sensitive topics.

**3. Reciprocity** — US social media companies (Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Twitter/X) are banned in China. TikTok is not banned in the US. The asymmetry is real, and both sides know it.

MySpace's Tom Anderson invented the "data as product" model in 2003, saw his company fail, and watched a Chinese company perfect his concept into a geopolitical flashpoint.

## FUTURE: The Splinternet

> ⚠️ Speculative — based on current regulatory trends across multiple countries.

The TikTok situation is one symptom of a larger structural change in global tech: **the fragmentation of the internet by national jurisdiction**.

The "splinternet" — the fracturing of the global internet into national or regional internets with different rules, different content, and different corporate ownership requirements — is already underway.

The EU's Digital Markets Act forces behavioral changes on US tech companies. Russia's sovereign internet infrastructure can disconnect from the global internet. China's Great Firewall is the most complete model. India has banned hundreds of Chinese apps.

**Prediction:** By 2030, the concept of a "global internet" will be largely aspirational. The technical internet will remain connected, but users in different countries will have meaningfully different experiences of what apps exist, what content is accessible, and who owns the infrastructure underneath.

TikTok's 14-hour ban in 2025 was a preview of what internet governance looks like when nations treat platforms as strategic assets rather than communications infrastructure. Tom Anderson's 2003 insight — that user behavioral data is a sovereign resource — took 20 years to become geopolitics.
