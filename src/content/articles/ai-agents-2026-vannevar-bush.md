## The Modern Story

In early 2026, something shifted in how AI companies talk about their products. The word "chatbot" quietly disappeared from press releases. In its place: **agent**.

Anthropic shipped Claude with computer use — the ability to operate a mouse and keyboard, browse the web, write and execute code, and complete multi-step tasks without human input at each step. OpenAI launched Operator. Google released Project Mariner. Microsoft embedded Copilot agents into Windows itself.

The pitch is simple and radical: you describe a goal, the AI figures out the steps, executes them, checks its own work, and reports back when done. You don't prompt it — you assign it.

A marketing team at a mid-size company in Manila can now tell an AI agent: *"Research our three main competitors, pull their pricing pages, draft a comparison table, and email it to me by noon."* The agent does it. No human oversight at each step. No checking in.

This is not a chat interface with better memory. This is something structurally different — and it was first described, in precise detail, by a man named Vannevar Bush in the summer of 1945.

## ROOT: Going Back to 1945

Vannevar Bush was not a visionary dreamer. He was a wartime administrator — the director of the US Office of Scientific Research and Development, the man who coordinated 6,000 American scientists during World War II, including the teams that built radar, proximity fuses, and the early atomic bomb research.

In July 1945, with the war ending, Bush published an essay in *The Atlantic* called **"As We May Think."** It was not a technical paper. It was a challenge: now that science had spent five years building weapons, what should it build next?

Bush described a hypothetical device he called the **Memex** — a mechanized personal desk that stored books, records, and communications, and could be retrieved "with exceeding speed and flexibility." But the Memex's most radical feature wasn't storage. It was **associative indexing** — the ability to link any item to any other item, and to follow chains of thought automatically.

The Memex could be instructed to *find* and *connect*. It anticipated the goal and handled the path.

**The chain from Memex to AI agents:**
- **1945** — Bush describes associative, goal-directed information retrieval in "As We May Think"
- **1968** — Douglas Engelbart demonstrates the oNLine System (NLS) at "The Mother of All Demos" — mouse, hypertext, collaborative editing
- **1987** — Apple's Knowledge Navigator concept video shows a voice-commanded agent scheduling meetings and retrieving documents
- **2011** — Siri launches: voice commands, limited task execution, no multi-step reasoning
- **2023** — AutoGPT goes viral: first public demonstration of an LLM autonomously chaining tasks and using tools
- **2024** — Claude computer use ships in beta: AI controls actual desktop software
- **2026** — Agents are commercial products from every major AI lab; enterprise deployments in production

Bush described the goal in 1945. The 81-year gap between the description and the product was almost entirely a compute problem.

## Did You Know

Bush's 1945 essay directly inspired **Douglas Engelbart**, who read it as a young radar technician in the Philippines and decided to dedicate his career to augmenting human intelligence with machines. Engelbart's 1968 "Mother of All Demos" — where he unveiled the mouse, video conferencing, and hypertext in a single 90-minute presentation — was explicitly framed as a response to Bush's challenge. Every computer interface built since is a direct descendant of that afternoon in San Francisco.

## Why It Matters Today

The arrival of AI agents is not a new idea becoming real. It is the end of an 81-year engineering problem that Bush correctly identified in 1945: the bottleneck was never the concept. It was the machinery.

What changes when agents become mainstream isn't just productivity. It's **the definition of a knowledge worker's job.**

When an agent can do the retrieval, the comparison, the first draft, and the error-checking — the human role shifts from executor to director. This is what Bush imagined: a scientist who could "call up" any document, make connections across disciplines instantly, and "consult" the record of human knowledge as easily as consulting memory.

The optimistic version: this frees humans for genuinely creative and judgment-heavy work.
The realistic version: it also eliminates large categories of entry-level knowledge work — the jobs that existed precisely because retrieval, comparison, and first drafts needed human hands.

Bush's Memex was supposed to augment the human mind. AI agents can replace significant portions of it. He didn't describe that part.

## FUTURE: The Agent Economy

> ⚠️ Speculative — based on current enterprise deployment trajectories.

Within 18 months, the most consequential question in enterprise technology will not be *which AI model is best* but *which agent orchestration layer controls the others.*

The pattern is already forming: individual agents (coding, research, customer service) are being wrapped into **agent networks** — systems where one AI directs others, checks their outputs, and escalates to humans only on failure or ambiguity.

**Prediction:** By 2028, most knowledge-work companies will have more AI agents running daily tasks than human employees handling them. The labor market disruption will not look like mass unemployment — it will look like the same headcount doing 3–5x the output, with middle layers of coordination work quietly disappearing.

Vannevar Bush described a world where scientists could finally think without the friction of retrieval. The agents arriving in 2026 remove that friction for everyone — at a scale, and a speed of deployment, that no postwar essayist could have predicted.
